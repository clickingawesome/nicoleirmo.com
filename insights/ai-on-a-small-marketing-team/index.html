What AI is actually good for on a small marketing team

Growth3 min read2026-09-03By Nicole Irmo

I lead AI adoption for a nine-person team. Here's what has earned its place, what hasn't, and the rule that keeps it honest.

Every vendor promises that AI will replace half your team. On a nine-person marketing and ecommerce team, that isn't the useful question. The useful question is which hours AI gives back and what we do with them. Framed that way, the answer is clear, and it isn't what the vendors are selling.

What has earned its place

First drafts of the tenth version of something. When you have written nine product descriptions in a voice, a model can write a credible tenth for a human to fix. Research summaries, especially of long documents nobody was going to read end to end. Variant copy for testing, where you need six honest attempts at the same headline. And the unglamorous formatting work that used to eat afternoons: resizing, retitling, restructuring, transcribing.

None of that's strategy. All of it was time. Getting the time back is the win.

What hasn't

Anything that requires knowing our customer better than a model can, which is most of the strategy and all of the judgment. The customer stories that make our marketing work were told by real people to a team that listened. A model can summarize them. It can't have heard them.

Anything customer-facing that ships without a person reading it. We tried, briefly. The errors were rare and mortifying, which is the worst combination.

The rule is simple. AI can draft, but a person owns. Nothing ships without a name on it.

What leading adoption actually involved

Selecting the platforms, which mostly meant saying no to eleven of them. Setting usage standards so the team knows what's fine and what isn't, in writing, with examples, because "use your judgment" isn't a policy. And building the workflows that turn a tool into a habit, which is where most teams stall. A tool nobody has a workflow for is a subscription, not a capability.

The standards were the hard part and the important part. They cover what can go into a tool (nothing about a customer, ever), what must be reviewed by a person (anything with our name on it), and what we tell people (we don't pretend a person wrote what a person didn't write). Simple rules, kept.

What it changed

More time for the puzzle. Less time on the parts of the job nobody went into marketing to do. The team is still nine people. They just get to spend more of the week on the work that needs a human, which is the only part that moves the numbers anyway.

If you lead a small team and you're being told to "do something with AI," start with the afternoons. Find the hours that are pure production and give those back first. Leave the judgment alone. That's the part you hired people for.
