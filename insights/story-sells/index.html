Story sells: turning a 20-year-old product catalog into a brand

Brand3 min read2026-08-12By Nicole Irmo

The company had two decades of products and a website. What it didn't have was a story anyone could repeat.

When I joined, marketing meant website support. The products were excellent, the customers were loyal, and nobody outside the company could say in a sentence what the brand stood for. That is not a marketing problem. That is a story problem, and you cannot fix a story problem with a bigger ad budget.

I know this because we tried the shortcuts first, briefly. More content. More promotions. The numbers twitched and settled back. The problem was not volume. The problem was that we had not decided what to say.

Position first, then everything else

We started with positioning: who the product is for, what it does that nothing else does, and why that matters at 11pm to a person who would rather not be thinking about it at all. Then we wrote the voice, the way the brand talks when it is being itself. Then a messaging architecture with standards for each stage of the funnel, so a product page and a television spot and a customer email all sound like the same company.

Only after that did we scale content, from ad hoc requests to more than forty published pieces a month. Volume is easy once you know what you are saying. Volume before you know is just noise, and expensive noise.

A brand people cite, share and buy from is not built by producing more. It is built by deciding what to say and then saying it everywhere.

What "everywhere" means in practice

It means the product page answers the same three questions the ad raised. It means the email a customer gets after ordering sounds like the person who wrote the ad, not like a shipping system. It means the customer service team has the same words we use in marketing, because we gave them the words. It means the Amazon listing, the blog, the video and the sample program all tell one story from different angles.

Consistency is boring to talk about and it is the entire game. Every place the story is told slightly differently is a place the customer has to do the work of reconciling it, and customers do not do that work. They leave.

The results followed the story

Organic search grew 32% in a year the industry declined 2.5%. Conversion on blog traffic went from 3.1% to 15.3%, because the blog stopped being a library and became part of the store. Revenue grew from $28.6M to $43.8M in under three years, through tariffs, a move to U.S. manufacturing and AI-driven search disruption.

$28.6M → $43.8Min under three years.

I want to be careful about the causality here. The story did not sell the product by itself. Excellent products sold the product. What the story did was remove the friction between a person having a problem and a person understanding, quickly, that this was the answer. That friction was costing us years.

If you are inheriting a marketplace

Resist the urge to redesign first. Write the story first. A redesign without a position is expensive decoration. A position without a redesign still sells, and it tells you exactly what the redesign should do when you get to it.

Then say it everywhere, including the places nobody thinks of as marketing. The packing slip. The hold music. The way the phone is answered. If it touches the customer, it is telling the story, whether you wrote it or not.
